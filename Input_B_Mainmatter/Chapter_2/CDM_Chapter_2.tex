\section{Ramsey's Theorem (The Classical Version)}

The following is regarded as the most important principle of discrete mathematics.

\begin{theorem}[Pigeonhole Principle (general form)]
\label{T:Pigeonhole Principle}
If $A, B$ are sets such that the cardinality of $A$ is strictly greater than the cardinality of $B$, then no function from $A$ into $B$ can be injective.
\end{theorem}

This principle is proved formally using the axioms of set theory, so we will not prove it here, and instead, we shall take it as a given fact. It should be intuitively clear.

The pigeonhole principle is informally stated in terms of pigeons and pigeonholes: If every pigeon must be given a pigeonhole, but there are more pigeons than pigeonholes, then at least one pigeonhole corresponds to more than one pigeon. 

An immediate corollary of Theorem~\ref{T:Pigeonhole Principle} is the following:

\begin{theorem}[Pigeonhole Principle (infinite form)]
\label{T:Infinite Pigeonhole Principle}
If $A$  is infinite and $B$ is finite, then there is $b\in B$ such that $f^{-1}(b)$ is infinite.
\end{theorem}


\begin{exercise}
Prove the preceding theorem.
\end{exercise}


In this section, we start to study multidimensional versions of the pigeonhole principle. To state them, we will need the notion of \emph{graph}.




Intuitively, a graph is a pictorial representation of a network, i.e., a collection points, along with line segments connecting some of those points. The points represent the nodes of our network, and a line segment connecting two given points represents an interconnection of the network between the nodes represented by those two points. The interconnections between points of a graph may be directional, in which case the graph is said to be a \emph{directed} graph, and the line segments are pictured as arrows. A directed graph represents flow: an arrow from point $a$ to point $b$  represents network flow from the node $a$ to the node $b$ (for example, if $a$ and $b$ represent cities, an arrow from $a$ to $b$  can be used to represent flow of information, flow of electricity, or flow of traffic from city $a$ to city $b$). If a graph is non-directed (which is the default case), a line segment between point $a$ and point $b$ represents a nondirectional connection between $a$ and $b$ (for example, if $a$ and $b$ represent persons, a nondirectional edge between $a$ and $b$ can be used to represent that $a$ and $b$ are acquaintances, or that they attended the same high school). Historically, graph theory was considered to be a subarea of geometry; for this reason, the points of a graph $\SG$ are historically referred to as the \emph{vertices} of $\SG$, and the line segments connecting points of $\SG$ are called \emph{edges} of $\SG$.

The formal definition of graph is as follows:

\begin{definition}
A \emph{directed graph} is a structure of the form $\SG=(G,R)$, where $G$ is a set called the set of \emph{vertices} (or \emph{nodes}) of $\SG$ and $R$ is a binary relation on $G$ called the \emph{adjacency relation} of $\SG$. The elements of the adjacency relation $R$ are called the \emph{edges} of $\SG$. Thus, the edges of $\SG$ connect vertices of $\SG$. If $\SG=(G,R)$ is a directed graph, two vertices $a, b$ of $\SG$ are \emph{incident} if $a\mathrel{R}b$, i.e., if they are connected by an edge of $\SG$.

When the adjacency relation $R$ is symmetric and antireflexive, we call $\SG$ simply a \emph{graph} (in other words, we drop the word ``directed''). 

If $\SG=(G,R)$ is a graph, a \emph{subgraph} of $\SG$ is any graph of the form  $\SG_0=(G_0,R_0)$, where $G_0\subseteq G$ and $R_0$ is the restriction of $R$ to $G_0$.

A graph $\SG$ is \emph{complete} if any two vertices of $\SG$ are incident (i.e., any two vertices of $\SG$ are connected by an edge of $\SG$). If $n$ is a positive integer and $G=\{1,\dots, n\}$, the complete graph  $\SG=(G,R)$ is denoted $K_n$. In other words, $K_n$ is the complete graph on $n$ points.

\end{definition}

The graph $K_{\BbN}$ contains the integers $0, 1, 2, \dots$ as vertices, with every pair $(m,n)$ of distinct integers  connected by and edge. Since the incidence relation is symmetric, it is useful to identify $K_{\BbN}$ with the set of all pairs of integers $(m,n)$ satisfying $m<n$. This identification is standard in the mathematical literature.

\begin{definition}
Given a graph  $\SG=(G,R)$, a \emph{coloring} of $\SG$ is a function $f$ that to each edge of $G$ assigns an element of a set $C$, which, in this context, we call a set of \emph{colors}. If the set $C$ has $m$ elements, we say that $f$ is a coloring of $\SG$ into $m$ colors. If $f$ is a given coloring of $\SG$, a subgraph  $\SG_0=(G_0,R_0)$ of $\SG$ is said to be \emph{monochromatic} under $f$ if the restriction of $f$ to $R_0$ is constant, i.e., if $f$ assigns the same color to all the edges of $\SG_0$.
\end{definition}


In general, a coloring of a set $A$ into $m$ colors, say, Color 1,\dots , Color $m$, is a function 
$f \colon A\to \{\text{Color 1},\dots, \text{Color $m$}\}$. This induces a partition of the set $A$ into $m$ mutually disjoint classes, namely, $f^{-1}(\text{Color 1}),\dots, f^{-1}(\text{Color $m$})$. Thus, any coloring function $f$, as above, partitions the set $A$ into equivalence classes, and each ``color'' in the range of $f$ provides a mathematical way to place a label on one of the classes.  


\begin{theorem}[Ramsey's theorem, classical version]
\label{T:Ramsey classical}
For every pair of positive integers $m,n$, there exists a least positive integer $R_m(n)$ satisfying the following property: For every coloring $f$ of the complete graph $K_{R_m(n)}$ into $m$ many colors, there is a copy of $K_n$ inside $K_{R_m(n)}$ that is monochromatic under $f$.
\end{theorem}


\begin{notation} 
To simplify notation, if the number $m$ of colors is 2, we denote $R_m(n)$ simply as $R(n)$.
\end{notation}

A popular interpretation of the existence of $R(n)$ is the following: Given any $n$, there exists $R(n)$ such that every party of $R(n)$  people contains a set of $n$ individuals who are either mutual acquaintances  or complete strangers.


Theorem~\ref{T:Ramsey classical}, proved by F. P. Ramsey (not to be confused with Michael Ramsey, his brother, who was the Archbishop of Canterbury, or with Arthur Ramsey, his father, who was a prominent mathematician and president of Magdalene College)~\cite{Ramsey:1929}, has given rise to a very active line of research in mathematics known as \emph{Ramsey theory} (or \emph{partition theory}), in which the goal is to prove that if a given structure (not only a graph, but say, a group, a matrix algebra, or a Banach space) is sufficiently large, then we are guaranteed to find in it a substructure (respectively, a subgroup, a subalgebra, or a subspace)  that is ``organized'' within the original structure in a recognizable way.

\begin{enumerate}

It is immediate that this configuration contains no  all-Red triangle or all-Green triangle.

\section{Ramsey's Theorem (An Infinitary Version)}


In order to prove Theorem~\ref{T:Ramsey classical}, we will first prove a simple infinitary version that can be informally stated as follows: ``Every party of infinitely many people contains infinitely many individuals who are either mutual acquaintances  or complete strangers.'' The formal statement is below.


\begin{theorem}[Infinitary version of Ramsey's theorem]
\label{T:Ramsey infinitary}
Suppose that the set of all pairs $(m,n)$ of positive integers with $m<n$ is partitioned into finitely many classes, or colors, say, Color 1, \dots,  Color $k$. Then there is an infinite  increasing sequence
\[
n_0 < n_1 < n_2<\dots < n_k<\dots
\]
of positive integers such that all the pairs of the form
\[
(n_i, n_j),\qquad
i<j
\]
fall within the same class (color).
\end{theorem}

Before proving Theorem~\ref{T:Ramsey infinitary}, let us observe an application to the theory of partially ordered sets:

\begin{example}
Let $(P,\le)$ be a partially ordered set (see Definition~\ref{D:posets}). 
Two elements $a, b$ of $P$ are said to be \emph{comparable} if $a\le b$ or $b\le a$. A subset $C\subseteq P$ is called a \emph{chain} if any two elements of $C$ are comparable, and an \emph{antichain} if no two elements of $C$ are comparable. By Theorem~\ref{T:Ramsey infinitary}, every infinite partially ordered set contains either an infinite chain or an infinite antichain.
\end{example}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{T:Ramsey infinitary}]
Fix a coloring of all the pairs $(m,n)$ for $m<n$ into finitely many colors.
By the infinite pigeonhole principle (see \ref{T:Infinite Pigeonhole Principle}), we can fix an increasing sequence
\[
n_0 ^0< n_1^0 < n_2^0<\dots < n_k^0<\dots
\]
of $(n)_{n\ge 0}$ such that the set of all pairs of the form
\[
(n_0 ^{0} ,n_j ^{0}),\qquad
j>0
\]
is monochromatic. Now we can find an increasing  subsequence
\[
 n_1^1< n_2^1<n_3^1<\dots < n_k^1<\dots
\]
of $(n_j ^{0})_{j\ge 1}$ such that the set of all pairs of the form
\[
(n_1 ^{1} ,n_j^1),\qquad
j>1
\]
is monochromatic. We continue this construction recursively: Given a subsequence
\[
 n_u^u < n_{u+1}^u<n_{u+2}^u<\dots <n_k^u<\dots
\]
of $(n_j ^{u-1})_{j\ge u}$ such that each of the sequences
\[
(n_0 ^{0} ,n_j ^{0})_{j>0},
\dots,
(n_{u-1}^{u-1} ,n_{j} ^{u-1})_{j>u-1}
\]
are monochromatic, we construct a subsequence
\[
 n_{u+1}^{u+1}< n_{u+2}^{u+1}<n_{u+3}^{u+1}<\dots < n_k^{u+1}<\dots
\]
of $(n_j ^{u})_{j\ge u+1}$ such that each of the sequences
\[
(n_0 ^{0} ,n_j ^{0})_{j>0},
\dots,
(n_{u-1}^{u-1} ,n_{j} ^{u-1})_{j>u-1},
(n_{u}^{u} ,n_{j} ^{u})_{j>u},
\]
are monochromatic.
Consider now the ``diagonal'' sequence $(n^*_u)_{u\in \mathbb{N}}$ defined by
\[
n^*_u=n_u^u,
\]
for all $u\in\BbN$. By construction, for each $u\in\BbN$, the sequence
\[
\tag{*}
(n^*_u, n^*_j)_{j>u}
\]
is monochromatic. 

Now, since the number of colors is finite and the number of possible $u\in\BbN$ above is infinite,  we can invoke the infinite pigeonhole principle once again, and then,  by replacing    $(n^*_u)_{u\in \mathbb{N}}$ by an adequate subsequence if necessary, we can assume that all the subsequences  in $(*)$ are the same color, regardless of $u\in\BbN$. This proves the theorem.
\end{proof}

\section{Real-Valued Relations and a Real-Valued Version of Ramsey's Theorem}
\label{S:Real valued}



\begin{definition}
\label{D:Relation}
Let $A$ be a set and $n$ a nonnegative integer. A \emph{$[0,1]$-valued $n$-ary relation} on $A$ is a function $R\colon A^n\to[0,1]$.
\end{definition}


\begin{theorem}[Infinitary version of Ramsey's theorem]
\label{T:Ramsey real-valued}
Suppose that $f$ is a function that assigns to every pair $(m,n)$ of positive integers with $m<n$ a ``color'' $f(m,n)\in[0,1]$. Then there exists a sequence 
\[
n_0 < n_1 < n_2<\dots < n_k<\dots
\]
and a ``limit color'' $c\in[0,1]$ with the property that
\[
\lim_{\overset{i,j\to\infty}{i<j}} f(n_i, n_j) =c.
\]
The latter means that for every $\epsilon>0$ there exists $N=N(\epsilon)>0$ such that
\[
\forall i,j \; (\,N<i<j 
\quad \Longrightarrow\quad
f(n_i, n_j)\in(c-\epsilon,c+\epsilon)\,).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Fix $f$ as in the statement of Theorem~\ref{T:Ramsey real-valued}. Split the interval $[0,1]$ as a union of two closed subintervals, say, $[0,1]=I\cup J$, with $I$ and $J$ of length $\frac{1}{2}$. By Theorem~\ref{T:Ramsey infinitary}, there exists a sequence
\[
n_0^0 < n_1 ^0< n_2^0<\dots < n_k^0<\dots
\]
such that $f(n_i^0, n_j^0)\in I$ for all $i<j$, or  $f(n_i^0, n_j^0)\in J$ for all $i<j$. In the former case, let $I_0=I$ and in the latter case, let $I_0=J$. Now we continue this process recursively:
For $u=1,2,\dots $, we construct sequences 
\[
n_0^u < n_1 ^u< n_2^u<\dots < n_k^u<\dots
\]
and subintervals $I_u$ of the unit interval such that for all $u\in\mathbb{N}$,
\begin{enumerate}
\item
$(n_j^{u+1})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}$ is a subsequence of $(n_j^u)_{j\in\mathbb{N}}$,
\item
$I_{u+1}$ is a subinterval  of $I_u$ of length $\frac{1}{2^{u+1}}$,
\item
$f(n_i^u, n_j^u)\in I_u$ for all $i<j$.
\end{enumerate}
Let $c\in [0,1]$ be such that
\[
c\in \bigcap_{u\in\mathbb{N}} I_u,
\]
and for each $u\in\mathbb{N}$ , define
\[
n_k=n_k^k.
\]
Then, $(n_k)_{k\in\mathbb{N}} $ has the desired property.
\end{proof}

Notice that in the preceding proof, the interval $[0,1]$ can be replaced by any closed interval $[a,b]$.
